## 2. Bug #20 was fixed on four of six columns

The fix from the previous session added `+ 0.0` — which collapses negative zero —
to `ci_low`, `ci_high`, `ci_low_dl` and `ci_high_dl`. It was **not** applied to
`pi_low`/`pi_high`, the prediction-interval bounds added in the same commit. Two
genes shipped it:

| Gene | Species | `pi_low` | `pi_high` |
|---|---|---|---|
| `APPL1` | human | −1.2733 | **−0.0** |
| `Ckap2l` | mouse | −1.6491 | **−0.0** |

That prints as "−0.000" in the readout and the forest-plot tooltip, and compares
as *not less than zero* in JavaScript — exactly bug #20's failure, on the column
added to fix bug #20's neighbourhood.

**Why the test did not catch it.** It named its columns literally:

```python
assert not (row["ci_low"] == 0 and math.copysign(1, row["ci_low"]) < 0)
assert not (row["ci_high"] == 0 and math.copysign(1, row["ci_high"]) < 0)
```

> **A test that enumerates its own columns only ever covers the bug that was
> already known.** It cannot fail on a column that did not exist when it was
> written, and a new interval column is exactly the thing most likely to repeat
> the defect.

**The fix.** One `_bound()` helper that every bound routes through, a
`BOUND_COLUMNS` tuple naming all six, and a test that loops over the tuple rather
than over hand-written names. The fixture gained a gene whose *prediction* bound
lands on zero, derived from the t quantile rather than hard-coded so it stays on
target if the interval's degrees-of-freedom convention moves.

**Verified in both directions**, per the previous session's own lesson: the bug
was reintroduced, the test failed with `pi_high shipped as negative zero for PI`,
the fix was restored, and it passed. The regenerated artifact carries negative
zero on no column.

## 3. Research — and two findings that changed the plan

Full document, with sources: [`VISUAL_AND_STATISTICAL_PLAN.md`](VISUAL_AND_STATISTICAL_PLAN.md).

### 3.1 This corpus is not a literature meta-analysis, and that inverts the priorities

Measured, not assumed. *k* is a median of 11 (IQR 10–14, max 16) and **31,976 of
41,983 genes are pooled from ten or more contrasts.** *k* varies because
microarray platforms differ in probe coverage — **not** because of what any study
found. Every gene is measured in every contrast that carries a probe for it.

Two consequences:

- **Selective outcome reporting is impossible by construction.** You cannot
  cherry-pick an outcome when the outcome is the whole transcriptome and the
  panel is rule-selected.
- **Every gene is pooled from very nearly the same 32 contrasts**, so per-gene
  influence aggregated over 41,983 genes answers *which of our 32 contrasts is
  bending the whole corpus.* That is a question about the panel itself, nothing
  in the repo can currently answer it, and it is the highest-value unbuilt item.

### 3.2 Egger's regression would have been wrong here

`ROADMAP` / `HANDOFF_2026-08-06.md` §6.2.3 lists Egger's test and a funnel plot as
priority 3. Two independent reasons demote it:

1. **Egger's test is miscalibrated on standardized mean differences.** The
   standard error of Hedges' *g* is a function of *g* itself, so effect and SE are
   correlated by construction and the regression finds asymmetry where none exists
   (Pustejovsky & Rodgers 2019). **The entire corpus is Hedges' *g*.** A naive
   implementation across 31,976 eligible genes would have produced thousands of
   confident false positives indistinguishable from findings.
2. **Publication bias is not the mechanism here** (§3.1), so funnel asymmetry
   cannot mean what it means in a literature synthesis.

It is not useless — reframed as a *design-confounding* diagnostic it is real,
because precision tracks tissue and platform across the 2.6× spread in contrast
SE. But the label a funnel plot normally wears would be false, and trim-and-fill
would impute studies that were never suppressed, in a repo whose first guarantee
is that nothing is asserted that was not measured.

## 4. The statistic that found a live defect

`geroquery/harmonize/shrinkage.py` — empirical-Bayes adaptive shrinkage across
the whole corpus (Stephens 2017), fit **per species, never pooled**: one shared
prior would let the mouse panel's spread shrink human estimates, which is a
cross-species claim smuggled in through a nuisance parameter.

Two columns come out of it, and both fix something.

### `lfsr` — the probability the *direction* is wrong

The question the tool is actually asked. Someone typing a gene wants to know
whether it rises or falls with age and how much to trust that; a p-value answers
"is the effect exactly zero", which nobody believes. The lfsr is defined for every
gene, including the 38,276 that currently get a verdict of `no_evidence` and
nothing else.

### `g_shrunk` — and the winner's curse that was shipping

The landscape table ranked by pooled *g* among genes whose interval excludes
zero. That selects on significance and then sorts on an extreme of a noisy
statistic. It is not a hypothetical:

| | Mouse table, former top rows | *g* | 95% CI | k | **lfsr** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `Kif21a` | +1.154 | [0.180, 2.127] | 10 | **0.461** |
| 2 | `Rrad` | +1.226 | [0.064, 2.389] | 12 | 0.445 |
| 3 | `Matr3` | +1.135 | [0.128, 2.142] | 13 | 0.435 |

**The row the mouse table led with had a 46% posterior probability of pointing
the wrong way**, with an interval that excludes zero. Of the 800 mouse genes the
table can draw from, **719 have lfsr > 0.2.** Human is better and not clean: the
former top row `ARHGAP36` sits at 0.150 while `SELENOM` (0.000) and `MT2A`
(0.005) ranked below it.

Corpus-wide: **3,707 genes have an interval excluding zero; 916 have lfsr ≤
0.05.** The two disagree because an interval is a statement about *one* gene and
the table is a selection over 41,983 of them. The lfsr carries that multiplicity;
the interval cannot.

> **Never rank a corpus-wide selection by a per-gene interval.** That is the
> whole defect, in one line.

### The estimator earns the extension

13 tests, on the same principle as the survey-weighted Cox path:

1. **A defining property.** Two genes reporting the same effect, one measured
   three times as precisely — the imprecise one must end up closer to zero. Fails
   if the posterior is not using the standard errors at all, which is a failure
   mode that leaves every summary looking fine.
2. **A structural identity.** A posterior mean under a prior unimodal at zero
   lies between zero and the estimate that produced it — never overshoots, never
   flips sign.
3. **Calibration against a planted truth.** Among genes called at lfsr ≤ *t*, at
   most *t* have the wrong sign, checked at 0.01, 0.05 and 0.10 against a
   simulation that knows the answer. **This is the only test a
   plausible-but-wrong posterior fails** — a mis-derived variance still returns
   numbers in [0,1] that fall as evidence strengthens and still plots fine.
4. **The winner's curse, made falsifiable.** On the top 500 raw estimates —
   exactly the rows the table shows — the shrunken estimator has lower mean
   squared error against the truth, and the raw estimates demonstrably overshoot.

> **A test premise that was wrong, recorded because it is instructive.** The
> planted-corpus test first asserted that `null_proportion` — the weight on the
> exact point mass — would recover the planted 0.90. It came out **0.66**, and
> that was the estimator being right. The grid's narrowest components sit ten
> times below the smallest standard error, so "exactly zero" and "very nearly
> zero" have almost identical likelihoods and the mixture cannot apportion mass
> between them; the point mass is identified only up to that neighbourhood. The
> API changed with the test: `prior_mass_below()` integrates the fitted prior over
> an interval, which *is* identified, and recovers 0.906 against a true 0.915.
> `null_proportion` now documents in its own docstring that it is not the null
> fraction.

## 5. The dark theme had an accessibility defect, and only a tool would find it

The palette is a diverging scale — `--up` warm, `--down` cool, `--null` neutral —
which is correct in structure. Run through a validator in both themes:

| Theme | Pair | Normal-vision ΔE | CVD worst | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Light | `--null` ↔ `--down` | 20.7 | 19.0 protan | pass |
| **Dark** | **`--null` ↔ `--down`** | **12.8** | 12.7 deutan | **FAIL, floor is 15** |

**In dark mode, "interval crosses zero" and "interval below zero" were hard to
tell apart with full colour vision** — two of the three verdicts, on the forest
plot and the landscape histogram, where that distinction is the product's entire
thesis. Light mode passed, which is why nobody saw it.

Fixed by search rather than by eye: `--null: #7d7568`, ΔE **19.1** normal / 11.8
protan / 18.9 tritan, contrast ≥ 3:1 on the dark surface. It is very nearly the
light theme's warm gray — the tell being that the neutral had been lightened
along with the rest of the ramp when it should have stayed put.

Two validator FAILs remain on this palette (lightness band, chroma floor) and are
**correct to ignore**: they are categorical checks applied to a diverging scale,
where a low-chroma neutral midpoint is required. Recorded so the next person does
not "fix" the midpoint by saturating it.

### The Certainty view

Effect size on x, −log₁₀(lfsr) on y, coloured by verdict, every pooled estimate
drawn. It exists because the landscape histogram bins effect size alone and hides
that the largest effects are the least certain.

**It deliberately offers no "large and significant" selector.** Selecting features
by effect size *and* significance does not control FDR — Benjamini-Hochberg
bounds the error rate over all features, not over a subset carved out with a
second filter. The guide lines read; they do not filter. That convenience is the
bug, and it is the reason the y-axis is an lfsr and not a p-value.

Labelled points are the **most certain**, not the most extreme. Labelling the
extremes would be the winner's curse drawn in text.

## 9. Gotchas added this session

- **Route every interval bound through `_bound`, and assert over
  `BOUND_COLUMNS`.** A test that names its own columns covers nothing that
  arrives later. Bug #20's residual.
- **Never rank a corpus-wide selection by a per-gene interval.** Winner's curse.
  Rank on `g_shrunk`; show `lfsr` beside it.
- **`lfsr` is not a p-value.** It is a posterior under a prior fitted from the
  corpus, so it is corpus-relative — the same gene gets a different lfsr if the
  panel changes. It belongs behind the estimator version in the evidence baseline
  like every other pooled quantity.
- **`null_proportion` is not the null fraction.** Use `prior_mass_below()`.
- **Fit the corpus prior per species, never pooled across them.**
- **Validate a palette, do not look at it.** Both themes. Light passing is not
  evidence that dark passes; here it hid a below-floor pair for two of three
  verdicts.
- **Do not "fix" the diverging midpoint's chroma.** A neutral gray midpoint is
  required for a diverging scale; the categorical validator flags it correctly
  and the flag should be ignored.
- **Egger's regression is invalid on Hedges' *g*** without a sample-size-based
  predictor, and publication bias is not this corpus's failure mode anyway.
- **A JSX comment cannot sit inside a ternary branch.** `{rows.length ? ( {/* … */} <p>` is a
  parse error, and `npm run build` failing is easy to miss when its output is
  piped through `tail` — Playwright then tests a stale `dist` and fails
  somewhere unrelated.
- **Never run two Playwright suites at once.** They race on the `vite preview`
  port and produce four unrelated failures in `gene.spec.js` that vanish on a
  clean run.
